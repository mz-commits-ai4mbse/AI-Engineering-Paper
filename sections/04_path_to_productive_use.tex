\section{A Structured Path Toward Productive Use}
\label{sec:path}

The design principles described in Section~\ref{sec:design_principles} can be organized
into a structured sequence for designing an AI-supported engineering workflow. The
sequence is not intended as a universal implementation lifecycle. Instead, it makes the
main engineering decisions explicit before probabilistic AI becomes part of a productive
process.

Figure~\ref{fig:controlled_ai_workflow} summarizes this processing logic from
source-grounded information through probabilistic interpretation and explicit engineering
authority to a controlled engineering state and an engineering artifact that is
subsequently assessed.

\subsection{Define the Engineering Task and Intended Result}
\label{sec:path_task}

The starting point is the engineering task rather than the AI technology. The engineering
purpose, expected result, scope of AI support, and relevant acceptance criteria should be
defined first. This establishes which information is relevant, where interpretation may be
required, which decisions have engineering consequences, and how the resulting artifact
will later be assessed.

\subsection{Establish the Information Basis}
\label{sec:path_information}

The available information should then be classified according to its role within the
workflow. In the investigated architecture, \emph{Engineering Sources} provided the basis
for engineering statements, while \emph{Context Sources} supported interpretation and the
\emph{Processing and Orchestration Context} described conditions influencing processing.

The exact categories may differ for another task. What should remain explicit is which
information may justify an engineering statement and which information only supports its
interpretation or processing.

\subsection{Identify Where Interpretation Is Required}
\label{sec:path_interpretation}

The workflow should distinguish between information that can be processed through explicit
rules and information that requires semantic interpretation. Probabilistic AI is especially
relevant at the latter boundary, for example when natural-language descriptions,
inconsistent terminology, incomplete structures, or different abstraction levels have to be
interpreted before further engineering processing.

Its role at this stage is to provide candidate interpretations and decision evidence rather
than to establish engineering authority.

\subsection{Make Unresolved Information Explicit}
\label{sec:path_uncertainty}

Probabilistic interpretation may produce agreement, competing interpretations, incomplete
information, or unresolved ambiguity. These states should remain visible rather than being
silently completed by generation.

In the investigated architecture, \emph{Consensus}, \emph{Variance}, and
\emph{Shared Ambiguity} represented different outcomes of multi-perspective interpretation.
Other implementations may use different mechanisms, but they likewise need to define when
processing may continue and when unresolved information has to be retained, reviewed, or
escalated.

\subsection{Assign Engineering Authority}
\label{sec:path_authority}

Where an AI-generated interpretation can affect subsequent engineering work, the
corresponding decision boundary should be explicit. In the investigated architecture, the
\emph{Human Engineering Authority} decided whether a defined interpretation state could be
used as authorized engineering information. A separate \emph{Model Authority} addressed
structural placement and representation where required.

The exact role structure is task-dependent. The important point is that consequential
transitions remain attributable to a defined authority and a clearly identifiable
information state.

\subsection{Create a Controlled Engineering State}
\label{sec:path_controlled_state}

Once the relevant interpretation and authority decisions have been resolved, authorized
engineering information can be transferred into a controlled engineering state that
provides a stable basis for downstream processing.

In the underlying implementation, this function was realized by the \emph{Internal
Engineering Model} (IEM). The specific representation is implementation-dependent; its
general function is to separate authorized engineering information from the probabilistic
processing that produced its candidate interpretation.

\subsection{Apply Rules, Constraints, and Assessment}
\label{sec:path_rules}

Where engineering meaning and representation decisions have been sufficiently resolved,
downstream processing should become rule-bound wherever appropriate. Target-specific
constraints, representation rules, and technical validation can then be applied to the
controlled engineering state.

If required evidence, authority, or rules remain unresolved, the workflow should retain
that state rather than silently introduce new engineering meaning. Subsequent assessment
remains distinct from generation and may include structural or semantic checks, tool-based
validation, human review, and release criteria.

\subsection{Preserve Traceability and Integrate the Workflow}
\label{sec:path_integration}

The complete path from source information through interpretation and human decisions to the
resulting artifact should remain reconstructable. For multi-source workflows, this includes
preserving source-local provenance when information is integrated into a common engineering
state.

Productive use additionally requires organizational integration. Responsibilities, review
activities, quality gates, and release mechanisms need to fit the existing engineering
process, while the associated review and authorization effort must remain practicable as
processing volume increases.

The resulting sequence therefore moves from probabilistic interpretation toward controlled
engineering use: information is interpreted where necessary, consequential decisions remain
explicitly authorized, authorized content is transferred into a controlled state,
downstream processing becomes increasingly rule-bound, and the resulting artifact remains
traceable and separately assessed.
